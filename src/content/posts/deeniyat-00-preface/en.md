## In brief

The preface is short, but it sets out the whole philosophy of the book. Maududi argues that religious education had been teaching *how* to practise Islam before teaching *what* Islam is and *why* it should be accepted. Deeniyat is his attempt to reverse that order: first understanding and conviction, then practice.

## Who the book is for

- Written mainly for young people in the final classes of high school and the early years of college.
- General readers can benefit from it as well.
- An earlier edition had already been adopted into the senior-class syllabus of many madrasas across India. This edition was revised with additions and omissions the author felt were necessary.

## The problem he identifies

In the usual religious curriculum, most of the time went to the detailed rulings of fiqh: prayer, fasting, purification and similar matters. Beliefs received comparatively little attention, and what was taught did not take root. As a result, a student could finish their schooling without knowing:

- what Islam actually is, and what it asks of a person;
- why it asks these things;
- how its beliefs relate to everyday human life;
- what a person gains by accepting them, and what they lose by rejecting them;
- whether Islam simply demands that its beliefs be accepted on authority, or whether it offers evidence for their truth.

## His argument

1. **Rules depend on faith.** Nobody obeys commands they do not believe in, so teaching rulings to someone without conviction achieves little.
2. **Faith depends on understanding.** Faith becomes firm only when the beliefs behind it are properly understood.
3. **Wisdom before details.** Before explaining *how* to pray, explain what prayer is, why it was made obligatory, what it gives a person, and what is lost by neglecting it. Knowing the method only helps someone who already wants to pray. The same applies to every other ruling.

## The approach of this book

- It presents a summary of the teachings of the Quran.
- It follows the Quran's own way of reasoning, giving arguments rather than bare instructions.
- The author describes it as a new method of religious teaching, quite different from the older one and better suited to modern times, and he hopes it will open the way for more effective religious education.

The preface is signed *Abul A'la* and dated 14 Muharram 1361 AH (1 February 1942). Since that is just before the third edition (March 1942), it was most likely written for that edition and kept in the fourth.

## Points to reflect on

- Do I know *why* I believe what I believe, or only *that* I believe it?
- Which acts of worship do I perform out of habit, without understanding their purpose?
- If I were teaching a child about Islam, would I begin with rules or with meaning?
